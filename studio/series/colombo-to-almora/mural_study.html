From Colombo to Almora · style study after the reference video

One mural, drawn by hand

I studied the reference video frame by frame. What makes it work is not the marker alone: it is one continuous drawing that grows as the narration speaks, crowded with shaded objects that each name something in the sentence, with the artist's hand in shot. The study below rebuilds that grammar with original drawings for fourteen seconds of the Colombo lecture, plus the closing quote card. The switches under the stage are the options.

One continuous mural

No hard cuts in the whole video. When a drawing is finished the camera pans right into white space, and the last drawing stays half in frame so ideas visibly join. A clean board appears only between big sections.

The hand is in the shot

The artist's hand and marker are on screen, drawing in time-lapse. It enters, draws, and leaves before the camera moves on.

Ink first, colour after

Each drawing is completed in black first, shaded with dense cross-hatching. Then saturated marker colour is scribbled in, and soft airbrush glows go round the important things.

Every object is shaded

Frames carry dozens of objects in collage: realistic hatched portraits, surreal literal metaphors, crowds of small things. Nothing is flat.

The words are on the board

Key sentences of the narration are hand-lettered in small capitals, with the important words in colour, plus labels, arrows and speech bubbles.

Colour carries meaning

Grey and black for the heavy, mundane world; rainbow light, spirals and sacred geometry for the moments of transcendence. A typeset quote card with a hatched portrait closes the film.

What it means for the films

A chapter is one mural

Each chapter becomes one continuous board that grows across its narration, about one drawing per one or two lines, and ends on a pull-back that shows the whole chapter. A new chapter starts on a clean board.

Collages from the text

The objects come from the sentence being spoken: Greek battalions, the Roman eagle, the spider's web on the Capitol, the ploughman's mark, the dynamo, the dew. More drawn things, fewer words.

Swamiji's words

His exact sentences are hand-lettered on the board as they are spoken, and the strongest one in each film closes on a quote card with his hatched portrait.

Portraits

The figures here are drawn from scratch. For the portraits of Swamiji, Ramakrishna and the people he meets, I can trace public-domain photographs from the 1890s into the same hatched ink. That needs a few photographs downloaded to your Mac.

Every frame is still drawn live from one timeline, so the films stay seekable, render to MP4 and play on a phone. All drawings here are original; the study borrows the reference's technique and grammar, not its artwork.
